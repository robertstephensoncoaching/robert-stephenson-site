Narrative · Identity · Leadership

What is Narrative Coaching, and how can it help a senior leader?

The stories we carry. The wants we uncover. The paths we could choose next.

Robert Stephenson PCC · 3 November 2026

You can change your job title overnight.

The story about who you are, what people expect of you and how you need to lead can take rather longer.

Perhaps you have become the person who always knows the answer. The one who holds everything together. The person who gets things done without asking for help.

Those ways of being may have served you well. They may even have helped you reach the role you hold today.

Then something changes.

Your responsibilities grow. A team needs something different from you. A relationship becomes difficult. You find yourself working harder, yet returning to the same uncomfortable place.

What happens when the story that brought you here has less to offer the person you are becoming?

That is one place Narrative Coaching can begin.

What is Narrative Coaching?

Narrative Coaching explores the stories through which we understand ourselves, other people and the situations we face.

By stories, I mean the meaning we give to experience.

“I have to be the strong one.”

“If I let go, things will fall apart.”

“They don’t want change.”

“I’m good at supporting other people. Taking the lead is something else.”

A story can hold experience, wisdom and something important to us. It can also narrow what we notice. We may begin to interpret each new situation through it, overlooking anything that doesn’t quite fit.

In coaching, we become curious about that.

Where did this understanding come from? What has it helped you do? What does it ask of you? What might it make difficult to see?

We can then explore other perspectives and ways of responding, with more awareness of the choices available.

Why does this matter for senior leaders?

The stories a leader carries can influence more than their own experience.

If I believe that being a good leader means having the answer, I may fill a silence before anyone else has had time to think.

If I see disagreement as a challenge to my authority, I may close down a conversation that could have helped the team.

If I believe I must protect everyone from uncertainty, I may take on responsibilities that other people are ready to share.

There may be good reasons for each response. The question is whether it serves the situation now.

Senior leadership brings responsibility, visibility and competing expectations. It can become difficult to distinguish what the role requires from what we have learned to require of ourselves.

Coaching gives us room to examine that relationship.

Who am I being in this role? What am I assuming others need from me? What have I stopped allowing myself to do?

What happens in a Narrative Coaching session?

We begin with something that matters to you.

It might be a conversation you are avoiding, a decision you keep circling, a new role or a team dynamic you cannot quite understand. You don’t need to arrive with a polished explanation.

As you speak, we pay attention to how you describe what is happening.

The language. The assumptions. The roles people seem to occupy. What feels possible, and what appears to have been ruled out already.

Two questions we might explore are:

What are we missing?

What is it we really want?

Sometimes we arrive quite certain about what we want. As we explore, another wish begins to emerge from beneath the layers of story, expectation and thinking.

You may want a promotion, and discover how much you are also longing for recognition. You may want to become better at managing a demanding role, and begin to acknowledge that you want your working life to feel different.

Both wants may be present. There may be tension between them. Something you have kept quiet, perhaps even from yourself, may need room to be heard.

We stay curious about what emerges. You decide what feels true and what it means for your choices.

We might stay with a phrase you use about yourself. Explore an experience from another person’s position. Notice what happens in your body as you describe a particular responsibility. Or consider a moment that doesn’t fit the account you have been giving.

My background in acting and physical theatre is part of what draws me to this work. I have spent years being curious about character, motivation, relationship and presence.

Who is this person being? What do they want? What are they protecting? What happens between them and the people around them?

In coaching, those questions meet your experience of leadership. You remain the person who decides what fits, what matters and what to do with what you discover.

Exploring the landscape

I sometimes think of this work as exploring a landscape.

From where you stand, one path may look like the only route available. You know its difficulties, but you also know where to put your feet.

Moving a little can change the view.

You may notice a path you had dismissed. A place you want to investigate. Someone whose perspective helps you see the terrain differently.

You may also decide that the path you are on is the one you want to take. Having explored, you can choose it with a clearer understanding of why.

That is part of what we do in a session. We look around together.

What else is here? What have we not yet considered? What might become visible from another position?

Some paths you will walk alone. Others involve your team, colleagues or people beyond work. Exploring your own story can change how you invite them into the conversation.

What could change?

Sometimes the shift begins in the way you understand your place in a situation.

“I’ve been put into this difficult situation” might open into a question:

“What can I contribute here?”

The difficulty may still be real. You may have limited authority, resources or influence. Coaching can help you recognise those limits while exploring where you do have room to act.

You might become more able to ask for support, hear disagreement or give someone else time to speak. You might notice that a familiar strength has become an obligation, and choose when to use it more deliberately.

Those shifts become useful through what you do next.

A different conversation. A clearer boundary. A decision you can explain. An experiment with your team.

We return to those actions and reflect on what happened. What changed? What surprised you? What still needs attention?

How do you know whether it is helping?

Insight matters, and we also need to consider its effect in your working life.

At the beginning, we explore what you want help with and what meaningful progress might look like. As the work develops, we revisit that.

Perhaps you are addressing conversations you previously avoided. Asking more useful questions. Sharing responsibility. Making decisions with greater clarity.

You may also notice that your understanding of the original challenge has changed. Something you thought was about confidence may turn out to involve belonging, expectations or a role you have outgrown.

We can examine that together and keep the work connected to what matters.

When might this approach be useful?

Narrative Coaching may be worth exploring when you are stepping into greater responsibility, navigating change or noticing a pattern that keeps returning.

It may also help when you can sense a gap between how you currently lead and how you want to show up.

You don’t have to know what a different story would be.

A place to begin is with a question:

What am I treating as the only possible story, and what might I discover if I looked around?
